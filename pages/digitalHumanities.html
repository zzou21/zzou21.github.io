Digital Humanities (DH) Projects

I combine my history research with computational methodologies and tools. The ultimate goal of digital humanities is not to create some sort of "super scholar" who has a PhD in history and PhD in computer science (although I won't be opposed to that). It is, instead, to build a mutually respectful and humble intellectual space where people from different disciplines actually listen to each other and try to see problems from other disciplines' perspectives rather than just wait for people from other disciplines to finish talking.

AsteXT: Asian American Literature

2023 - now

AsteXT Project Website (astext.oit.duke.edu).

I started the AsteXT project to study how the writing and language in Asian American short stories changed over time and to what extent, if quantitatively detectable, did these changes reflect major turning points Asian American history. This project has many goals: one is to create a publicly available semantic-search-enabled database of Asian American short stories between 1974 and 2024. The goal to pursue semantic search is to allow more users (especially students, secondary school teachers, artists, community members, artists, etc.) to more easily find Asian American short stories. Existing database structures requires the user to know at least the story title, author, publication year, and journal, which are information that only users previosuly familiar with the discipline know. Having semantic search allows users who are not familiar with the discipline of Asian American literature to still find short stories that they want for learning, teaching, or even personal enjoyment. To the best of my knowledge, this project creates the first public database focusing specifically on Asian American short stories.

Another goal is to answer specific research questions. One question is how did Asian American short stories perceive the idea of "space" in their literature. To do so, we focus on identifying non-named entities. For example, instead of finding simply the names of cities mentioned in a story, we also hope to identify just the word "city" as another component of a story's overall spatial construction. To answer this question, this project manually annotates short stories to train a domain-specific non-generative language model to detect more non-named entities at scale. Named Entity Recognition has been a field of research for many years, but few researchers have engaged with non-named entity recognition. Our work hopes to fill that gap.

  1. Built a publicly available relational database using a custom metadata structure by expanding what metadata a traditional archive might include, such as using spaces that the story is set in as another metadata.
  2. Collected over 550 Asian American short stories and performed Named Entity Recognition on them to find the spatial metadata.
  3. Built a customized data annotation tool to prepare data to train a domain-specific non-generative language models to identify non-space entities in future Asian American short stories.
  4. Built a BERT fine-tune workflow to fine-tune various BERT models into domain-specific spatial non-named entity recognition machines.
  5. Taught a year-long DH research tutorial seminar. See Teaching page for details.

I am the Principal Investigator for the AsteXT team along with my collaborator, Dr. Matthew Hayes. This team consists of over 15 members, ranging from faculty, staff, undergraduate students, and PhD students at Duke University. This is funded by multiple grants and financial supports. Primarily, it was funded by Duke University Bass Connections, Data+, and Code+ project grants, of which I was the first student to be awarded these grants, as they were historically reserved only for faculty or departments to run research teams. Other sponsors included Duke University Trinity College Deans' office, Duke University URS Office, and Duke University Asian/Pacific Studies Institute. In Summer 2025, funding that this team received created 9 full-time undergraduate DH research positions and 1 part-time PhD student DH research position.

Basic Tools

2025 - now

Using Electron, JavaScript, HTML, and CSS, I built an open-source desktop application called Basic Tools that peforms basic daily computer needs in our professional, work, research, family, or personal digital lives. Many of these tools are ones that I think major OS providers should have already provided in an easy-to-use manner but have not. Some of them include: quick bulk image format converter, quick bulk image renamer, quick bulk PDf merger, etc. The main difference with this app is that it stays completely locally to your device and does not require user account creation. This means that your document stays completely offline and only on your device, away from user account breaches, AI bots, unsecure free online tools, other potentially malicious actors online, or heavily expensive proprietary softwares like Adobe.

Download the app at Basic Tools's website here. The app supports both Windows and MacBook.

This project was supported by the Duke University Co-Lab Development Grant.

I was a researcher on Dr. Astrid Giugni (Duke University)'s "Ethical Consumption Before Capitalism" DH research team. Local XML-TXT Converter

2024-5

I developed a local converter that turns XML files into plain text TXT files using Python and TKinter. The tool was originally designed to target XML tags in the Early English Books Online (EEBO) database and turn their XML files quickly into TXT files. The tool currently allows users to customize which XML tags they want to remove and which to keep. This way, users are given the maximum flexibility instead of a blanket XML tag remover.

Autograder for Open-Ended Programming Assignment

2025-6

Under the supervision of Yesenia Velasco, Dr. Kristin Stephens-Martinez, and Dr. Susan Rodger, I researched the development of autograding softwares to grade open-ended programming assignments. Existing autograders are used mostly for close-ended quesions, such as multiple choice or applying existing formulas (e.g., converting Fahrenheit to Celsius). However, close-ended assignments do not foster students' creativity. Few autograders are able to sufficiently grade, and provide customized feedback, to students.

I developed three autograding softwares for open-ended Computer Science programming assignments. Two are used in Duke University Computer Science Department's Coursera MOOC class and one is used in CompSci Department's CS 101 class.

I used PyTest, Python Unit Test, Coursera API, Gradescope API, and similar tools in addition to design principals derived from current research in Computer Science education/pedagogy to develop these softwares.
